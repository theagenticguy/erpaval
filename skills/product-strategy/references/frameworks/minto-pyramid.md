# Minto Pyramid (thinking structure)

## Canonical structure

Minto's principle: readers absorb top-down. State the answer first, then the groups of reasons that support it, then the evidence under each reason. At every level, the supporting points are MECE — **m**utually **e**xclusive, **c**ollectively **e**xhaustive [8, 9].

**SCQA opening** sets up the answer:

- **S — Situation** — baseline context the audience already agrees on.
- **C — Complication** — the disruption or change that makes action necessary.
- **Q — Question** — the implicit question the complication raises.
- **A — Answer** — your recommendation, at the top of the pyramid.

**Hierarchy below the answer**: 3–5 grouped sub-points, each backed by evidence. Reading top-down lets an executive stop at any level and still have a coherent view — the hallmark of the structure.

**Difference vs long-form narratives**: an executive memo *is* a Minto pyramid serialized into narrative prose under length and format constraints (page count, banned bullets, silent-reading discipline, FAQ). Minto owns the thinking structure, not the document format — hand the pyramid to whatever narrative-writing workflow your team uses for the prose composition.

## When to use

Executive communication, consulting decks, strategy memos, PR-FAQs, any document where the reader is time-poor and decision-focused. Also useful as a *thinking tool* before writing: if you can't state the answer, you don't know the argument yet. Useful to rescue a memo that reads like a chronology or a brain-dump — re-pyramid it and the missing MECE gaps show up immediately.

## When to skip

Skip for exploratory writing where the conclusion isn't known yet — a research log, a discovery round, a brainstorm. Skip for creative or emotional content; Minto dulls narrative voice. Skip when the reader expects chronological storytelling — post-mortems, case studies, change logs. Skip when the real work is a Rumelt kernel; Minto structures the *output* of strategy, not the strategy itself.

## Template / worked example

```markdown
## Answer (top of pyramid)

[One sentence: the recommendation.]

## SCQA framing

- Situation: [what the audience already agrees on]
- Complication: [what changed, what's new, what's broken]
- Question: [the implicit question the complication raises]
- Answer: [repeat of top, 1-2 sentences expanding]

## Supporting argument 1 — [group label]

[Claim statement.]

- [Evidence / sub-claim]
- [Evidence / sub-claim]

## Supporting argument 2 — [group label]

[Claim statement.]

- [Evidence / sub-claim]
- [Evidence / sub-claim]

## Supporting argument 3 — [group label]

[Claim statement.]

- [Evidence / sub-claim]

## MECE check

- Mutually exclusive: do any two groups overlap? [name overlaps or state none]
- Collectively exhaustive: what's missing that a reader would ask? [close the gaps]
```

**Worked example excerpt**:

> Answer: fund the agent-evaluation platform team for H2, scope to two product lines.
> Situation: five teams are each building eval tooling.
> Complication: we cannot compare agent quality across products; customer incidents are landing without a shared rubric.
> Question: how do we get a shared rubric fast enough to matter?
> Three MECE groups supporting the answer: (1) cost — a shared team is ~30% cheaper than five parallel builds; (2) speed — a focused team can ship one rubric in H2 vs five partial rubrics; (3) risk — the alternative creates incompatible rubrics that will need a painful reconciliation later.

## Validation checks

- Answer is at the top, one sentence, and actually answers the implicit question.
- SCQA flows — situation is agreed, complication is real, question is natural.
- Sub-points are MECE at every level — no overlap, no gap.
- Each sub-point has specific evidence below it, not just rephrased claims.
- A reader can stop at any level and still get a coherent view.
